# AI-Powered Ransomware and Critical Infrastructure: What the NCSC Warning Means for the Board

The National Cyber Security Centre has assessed that it is highly likely that, between now and 2028, AI-powered malware will have been used in a ransomware attack on UK critical national infrastructure. The assessment appears in the NCSC's 2026 annual review and was set out by its chief executive, Richard Horne. For boards in energy, transport, defence, food, health and finance, this is a near-term operational concern that bears on resilience planning and risk acceptance.

This article explains what the NCSC said, how AI changes the ransomware threat, and what leadership should require of management now.

## What the NCSC assessed

The NCSC judges that AI is helping to industrialise cyber attacks and that AI-powered malware is highly likely to be used in a ransomware attack on UK critical national infrastructure before 2028. The UK's definition of critical national infrastructure spans sectors that include energy, transport, defence, food, health and finance, so the warning reaches a broad set of organisations.

The review also states that Russia and China are expected to use AI routinely in cyber activity. Horne warned of the prospect of an unprecedented rise in the number of cyber incidents in the coming months, and attributed much of the risk to AI's ability to find weaknesses in IT systems that have been tolerated for a long time. He pointed to recent attacks that raised the temperature of supermarket refrigeration in Europe as a signal for organisations to invest in resilience, and said communications networks used by the British military face persistent and sustained attacks, some attributed to Russia and deliberately kept below the threshold of armed conflict.

## How AI changes the ransomware threat

Ransomware follows a familiar pattern. An attacker gains access to an organisation's systems, encrypts files with malicious software, and demands a payment in cryptocurrency to restore access and to avoid the release of stolen data. UK organisations that have been publicly reported as ransomware victims include Royal Mail, the outsourcing firm Capita, and a number of public bodies.

AI affects several stages of this pattern. The NCSC describes AI helping attackers scout targets, locate weaknesses in IT networks, and write malicious code. Where these tasks become faster and cheaper, an attacker can identify and reach more victims in less time. The same capability has a defensive use. AI recently helped identify a critical weakness in PHP, a programming language used by a large share of the world's websites, which shows that the technology can surface flaws that have gone undetected. The risk the NCSC describes is one of pace, where attackers who gain access to capable models can act before defenders have patched the weaknesses those models expose.

For the board, the significance is not a new category of attack but a change in tempo. Where detection, patching and recovery depend on manual effort, a faster attacker shortens the time available to respond.

## What leadership should require

The board does not need to design controls, but it can require management to demonstrate a small number of things that bear directly on this threat.

Management should be able to show which parts of the organisation meet the national definition of critical national infrastructure, and which suppliers and systems those functions depend on. Where ransomware reaches infrastructure through a connected supplier or a shared service, the dependency map matters as much as the organisation's own estate.

Management should be able to evidence the controls that reduce ransomware impact, including how systems are segmented, how privileged access is restricted, and how quickly critical services can be restored from backups that an attacker cannot reach. These are capabilities to establish and test through exercises, confirmed by review.

Management should maintain a current view of unpatched weaknesses in internet-facing and business-critical systems, with a defined time to remediate the most serious. Where AI shortens the time between a weakness becoming known and an attacker exploiting it, the organisation's own speed of remediation becomes a control in its own right.

Management should rehearse the decisions a ransomware incident forces, including whether and how to continue operating degraded services, how to communicate with regulators and customers, and who holds the authority to act. Rehearsal turns a plan into a capability the board can rely on.

## Questions for the board

- Which of our functions fall within the national definition of critical national infrastructure, and which suppliers and systems do they depend on?
- How quickly can we restore our most critical services, and when did we last test recovery against ransomware that reaches our backups?
- What is our time to remediate the most serious weaknesses in internet-facing systems, and is it short enough given a faster attacker?
- How is privileged access restricted and monitored, and how would we detect an attacker moving towards our most critical systems?
- When did we last rehearse the executive decisions a ransomware incident forces, including degraded operation and regulatory notification?
- How do we assess the ransomware resilience of the suppliers our critical services depend on?
- Who owns this risk at executive level, and how does it reach the board?
